The problem

The signal already exists. Nobody's using it.

Every time you tap your loyalty card, the supermarket already knows you bought spinach six days ago. That's not a farm problem or a store problem — it's 30% of the country's food waste, sitting in home kitchens, after it's already been paid for.

"1.2 million tonnes of food are wasted in Aotearoa every year. We built the system that turns your own purchase history into the thing that stops your food becoming waste."

How the classifier works

Predicting why a household wastes, not just how much.

WasteWise doesn't guess that "this item is old." It classifies each household into one of six food-waste segments, based on the behavioural pattern behind their waste — then times and words the nudge to match that pattern.

The segment taxonomy comes from a real study of loyalty-card grocery data: households were surveyed on why their food gets wasted, then a decision tree was trained to predict those self-reported reasons from purchase history alone.

Segment taxonomy and classifier approach: Närvänen et al., 2023, International Journal of Consumer Studies (DOI 10.1111/ijcs.12903). Purchase-history feature families (shopping frequency, planning regularity, overprovisioning, discount sensitivity): Merian et al., 2022, Sustainability (DOI 10.3390/su141610183).

Some segments predict cleanly from purchase data alone — overpurchaser/overpreparer households, who buy in bulk and let perishables turn, score 0.91 F1. Others, like households who over-trust date labels, are attitudinal rather than behavioural, and predict weaker — a documented limitation of the underlying research, not a bug we're hiding.

The recovery hierarchy

Eat it, share it, compost it — in that order, on purpose.

This ordering isn't a WasteWise invention. It's the standard food recovery hierarchy used across waste management: prevent waste first, feed people second, and only divert to compost or landfill once both of those have failed.

The reason for the order is value, not tidiness. Eating the food recovers its full nutritional and economic value. Sharing it recovers most of that value for someone else. Composting only recovers the soil nutrients — everything else is already lost by that point. So the system escalates in that sequence, and never skips a step.

While shelf life remains Eat it

Recipe nudge, timed to what's about to turn.

Unopened / whole produce only Share it

Routed to a real, named pātaka kai nearby that accepts it.

Last resort Compost it

Council collection info, only once the first two have failed.

Sharing is deliberately narrow: only unopened, whole items — like uncut produce or shelf-stable packaged goods — are ever routed to a pātaka kai. Cut or opened perishables, dairy, meat and fish, and prepared meals are excluded outright, because the hierarchy only works if the "share it" step never puts anyone's safety at risk. That's a recovery hierarchy, not a reminder app.

Who runs this

A collaboration layer, not another app.

Each partner keeps what they already own. WasteWise owns the decision logic that connects them — each one plugged in independently over an open protocol, never centralised.

Supermarket Data & channel

Keeps the purchase data and the relationship with the shopper. Woolworths, or any Everyday Rewards-scale partner.

Non-profit Trust & voice

Love Food Hate Waste NZ authors the tone — so this never reads as retailer surveillance.

Council / pātaka kai Recovery

Handles what prevention couldn't catch — sharing networks and compost collection.

The "open protocol" is the Model Context Protocol (MCP) — a standard that lets a system like WasteWise call tools that another organisation hosts and owns, without that organisation ever handing over its underlying data. Love Food Hate Waste NZ runs its own MCP server for tips. The pātaka kai network runs its own MCP server for pantry locations. The council runs its own for organics collection. WasteWise never stores or copies their data — it calls in, gets an answer, and moves on.

The nudge platform is the classifier and the recovery-hierarchy state machine from earlier, wired to this protocol. The classifier reads a household's basket and assigns a segment; the state machine decides the tier — PREVENT, SHARE, or DIVERT. Only once that decision is made does WasteWise call out, and it's only allowed to call the one partner server scoped to that tier:

PREVENTlfhw-mcp.get_tip, foodkeeper-mcp.get_shelf_life, recipes-mcp.find_recipes
SHAREpatakakai-mcp.find_nearby_pantry
DIVERTcouncil-mcp.get_organics_service

That scoping is enforced in code, not just described in a diagram — a SHARE decision physically cannot reach the LFHW server, and a PREVENT decision cannot reach the council server. Nine automated tests confirm the scoping holds and that a nudge built from an MCP call passes the exact same validation as one built from local data.
